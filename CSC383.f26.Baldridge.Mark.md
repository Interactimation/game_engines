## Introduction

This is a new course, a sort of trial: The premise, proposed by Professor David Kopec, chair of the Division of Technology, Gaming & Math:

> An introduction to a number of game engines, emphasizing a vibecoding approach

It was this last bit that caught _my_ attention 

Here, then, are the elements of this course:

### Game Engines 

A game engine is a software development environment for video games. One may design sprite sheets in Photoshop, 3D models in Maya and the game they fit into in one of a number of "engines" 

I initially selected five different "game engines" for our perusal:

* **Game Maker:** A powerful 2D game engine, popular with students
* **Godot:** A free and open source engine, one you can own and modify, build into your own studio. An up-and-comer in the field, we are seeing more jobs with this qualifier
* **Unity:** Free to use —until your project earns $200,000 in given year, when you musth buy a license— one of the most powerful and popular engines out there 
* **Unreal:** The most powerful engine commercially available, it's free to use until your project garners $1 _million_, at which time the company will take 5%. The learning curve here is steeper than some others  
* **Babylon.js:** Chosen as an outlier, this is a javascript library for building web-based games from pure code and assets 

> You may have heard of or used one or more of these already. What makes this course different is the _vibe coding_ approach 

### Vibe Coding 

Vibe coding is working with AI tools to produce viable code 

Some may already be looking for the exits: AI remains unpopular with some students, and for good reason

> What appeals to me about developing this course is the opportunity to get beyond some of those reasons

Let's actually look at some of them here:

* **AI are resource sinks** 

Water and other resources _are_ gobbled up by giant data centers, butting strain upon local communities. The "per" prompt is perhaps far lower than you've heard, but the number of prompts is staggering!

> We'll begin, at least, by developing workflows around _local inferences_— these are smaller, open weight AI models build to run entirely on your own hardware. Some of them work on phones!

Such objects use only the power and resources you're already using on video games and cat pix

* **They hallucinate**

This likely won't change. In fact, it strikes me as very humanlike, to be proudly certain about something you've totally got wrong

> We will customize our local inferences with instructions and resource documents, telling them how to do the the things we'll ask them to do

Do be prepared for some frustrations, however. Think of them as the frustrations you have with _yourself_ when trying to learn to do something correctly...

* **Thy're difficult to guide and impossible to "art direct"** (asking for a minor change more often results in a completely new output)

> One of the things we'll discover are the limits of "collaboration" between humans and the current crop of AI 

But the models show no signs of leveling-off in their development. We'll find, over the course of the semester and throughout all future practice with AI models, that the terrain evolves beneath us!

_Today's limitations will not be tomorrow's_

* **The looming "bubble"** 

I took this question seriously, but what we should consider is that the bursting of a technological bubble does not always, or even often leave the technology with nowhere to go

_It's _companies_ that collapse_

> I chose the local inference with which I'll demonstrate these techniques with some care, thinking that "the bubble" might burst while we're still in session!

Gemma 4 is Google's latests local inference, it's free, private and available under the Apache 2.0 license. _It lets anyone use, modify, and share for any purpose, including commercial use, without paying fees or making their own modified code open source_

**A game or interaction you build today can be packaged with Gemma 4 running some aspect of its functioning, and sold**

While Anthropic and OpenAI are outspending their incomes many times over, Google can give away a product it cost billions to develop, and it's doing so for this very reason: _It can afford to_ 

> TO BE CLEAR: students may use other local inferences, even paid subscriptions to cloud-based AI services throughout this course

But from the beginning, I will use Gemma and, for some (mostly demonstrational) purposes, either ChatGPT or Claude's cloud-based service

**I'm interested in making the resulting AI studios free, private and useful for a range of applications**

## Goals and Objectives

The course is called Game Engines and that will be our target skills-based demonstration of what I consider the more challenging and interesting process of learning to use AI beyond the common "prompt and paste" model _causing so much grief in the world today!_

With that in mind:

### Instructional Goals: 

* Build, with students, a small AI studio to aid in a wide variety of endeavors, starting with work in game engines
* Explore the relation between student and model in creating viable projects
* Develop an agile approach to technological change, going forward

### Learning Objectives

* **Technical Proficiency** Rapidly prototype attractive interactions with a number of game engines using AI tools
* **Conceptual** Find the limits of collaboration with the AI model and build _a functional process that works within those limits_
* **Developmental** Discover the one true thing about technical creation: that learning is a lifelong endeavor

### Projects

In this course we will produce a small number of projects

Originally that number was five, produced in each of the five game engines I marked out at the beginning. That would give us only three weeks per engine, a good test of AI-based approaches, with no time to learn a new language for each!

However, in the process of preparing this course, I found the technology changing beneath me. We may see that creating and managing the AI studio(s) intended to help us in addressing game engines, may take first place

> In all the below examples, the use of found, stolen or adapted assets (sprite sheets, models, animations, etc) is encouraged!

**Let's imagine Three Projects**

* **GameMaker** 

Produce something that looks like a prototyped "level" with a player controlled sprite, "enemies" and obstacles

Includes user control, collisions, spawning (as of enemies) and possibly animations

**Alternatively** one could produce fully sprite-sheet-animated characters, and manipulate them around the scene picking up and using items in a puzzle or "escape room" scenario

* **Godot** 

Lets take this 3D and create either a similar "player controlled character with spawning enemies and obstacles" in a 3d environment 
OR
Some a "coin collecting" or other kind of gathering, puzzle or escape game

* **Some Third Application** 

This will likely be Unity or Babylon.js and I can see merits to each: Unity is not terribly different from Godot in functionality, a pure code-based approach like that offered by Babylon might be different enough to make a real spectrum of our game engines...

> In this as in most things: I'm open to suggestions!

## Evaluation

All course work will be evaluated on the basis of a 100-point semester

You can think of it as being organized in three sections, each focused on one Project

* **Three Projects**

* **The remaining 10 points are divided into:**

> If, early on, you have only one Creativity point, for example, be aware that this grade will not be finalized until the semester ends!
